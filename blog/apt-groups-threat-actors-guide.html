APT Groups & Threat Actors: Complete Intelligence Guide

📅 January 20, 2025 • ⏱️ 18 min read

Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) groups represent the most sophisticated cyber adversaries in the threat landscape. Understanding their tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) is essential for building effective defenses and threat intelligence programs.

What Are APT Groups?

APT groups are highly organized, well-funded cyber threat actors—typically nation-state sponsored—that conduct long-term, targeted campaigns against specific organizations or sectors. Unlike opportunistic cybercriminals, APTs focus on:

  • Strategic objectives: Espionage, intellectual property theft, or infrastructure disruption
  • Persistence: Maintaining long-term access to compromised networks
  • Stealth: Avoiding detection through advanced evasion techniques
  • Sophistication: Using custom malware and zero-day exploits

Key Characteristics:

  • Advanced: Sophisticated tools and techniques beyond typical cybercriminals
  • Persistent: Long-term campaigns spanning months or years
  • Threat: Significant risk to national security and critical infrastructure

Major APT Groups by Region

🇨🇳 China-Nexus APTs

APT1 (Comment Crew)

Attribution: People's Liberation Army (PLA) Unit 61398

Targets: 141+ organizations across 20 industries, primarily English-speaking countries

TTPs: Spear-phishing, custom backdoors (WEBC2, BACKDOOR.BARKIOFORK), credential theft

Notable: First APT group publicly attributed to specific military unit (Mandiant 2013 report)

APT10 (MenuPass, Stone Panda)

Attribution: Chinese Ministry of State Security (MSS)

Targets: Managed service providers (MSPs), aerospace, defense, healthcare

TTPs: Supply chain attacks via MSPs, PlugX malware, ChChes backdoor

Notable: Operation Cloud Hopper - massive MSP compromise campaign

APT41 (Double Dragon)

Attribution: Chinese state-sponsored + financially motivated

Targets: Healthcare, telecom, gaming, software companies globally

TTPs: Dual-purpose operations (espionage + financial crime), supply chain attacks

Notable: Unique hybrid model combining state espionage with cybercrime

🇷🇺 Russia-Nexus APTs

APT28 (Fancy Bear, Sofacy)

Attribution: Russian GRU (Main Intelligence Directorate)

Targets: Government, military, security organizations, media

TTPs: Spear-phishing, X-Agent malware, credential harvesting, influence operations

Notable: DNC hack (2016), Olympic Destroyer, targeting of anti-doping agencies

APT29 (Cozy Bear, The Dukes)

Attribution: Russian SVR (Foreign Intelligence Service)

Targets: Government agencies, think tanks, research institutions

TTPs: Stealth-focused, custom malware families (CozyDuke, MiniDuke), supply chain attacks

Notable: SolarWinds supply chain compromise (2020), COVID-19 vaccine research targeting

Sandworm (Voodoo Bear)

Attribution: Russian GRU Unit 74455

Targets: Critical infrastructure, energy sector, Ukraine

TTPs: Destructive attacks, ICS/SCADA targeting, wiper malware

Notable: Ukraine power grid attacks (2015, 2016), NotPetya ransomware (2017)

🇰🇵 North Korea-Nexus APTs

Lazarus Group (Hidden Cobra)

Attribution: North Korean Reconnaissance General Bureau (RGB)

Targets: Financial institutions, cryptocurrency exchanges, defense contractors

TTPs: Financially motivated attacks, destructive malware, cryptocurrency theft

Notable: Sony Pictures hack (2014), WannaCry ransomware (2017), $81M Bangladesh Bank heist

APT38 (BeagleBoyz)

Attribution: North Korean state-sponsored (Lazarus subgroup)

Targets: Banks, SWIFT infrastructure, cryptocurrency platforms

TTPs: Financial system intrusions, SWIFT manipulation, cryptocurrency theft

Notable: Specialized in bank heists and financial fraud operations

🇮🇷 Iran-Nexus APTs

APT33 (Elfin, Holmium)

Attribution: Iranian government-sponsored

Targets: Aviation, energy, petrochemical sectors in US, Saudi Arabia, South Korea

TTPs: Spear-phishing, Shamoon wiper malware, credential harvesting

Notable: Focus on Saudi Arabian infrastructure and aviation sector

APT34 (OilRig, Helix Kitten)

Attribution: Iranian Ministry of Intelligence and Security (MOIS)

Targets: Financial, government, energy, chemical sectors in Middle East

TTPs: DNS tunneling, custom backdoors (BONDUPDATER, POWBAT), credential theft

Notable: Extensive Middle East targeting, sophisticated persistence mechanisms

Common APT Tactics, Techniques & Procedures

Initial Access

  • Spear-phishing: Highly targeted emails with malicious attachments or links
  • Watering hole attacks: Compromising websites frequented by targets
  • Supply chain compromise: Infiltrating software vendors or service providers
  • Exploit public-facing applications: Targeting vulnerable web servers, VPNs

Persistence

  • Registry run keys: Malware auto-start via Windows registry
  • Scheduled tasks: Periodic execution of malicious code
  • Web shells: Backdoors on compromised web servers
  • Account manipulation: Creating hidden admin accounts

Privilege Escalation

  • Credential dumping: Extracting passwords from memory (Mimikatz)
  • Exploitation: Using privilege escalation exploits
  • Token manipulation: Impersonating privileged accounts

Defense Evasion

  • Obfuscation: Encoding/encrypting malicious code
  • Rootkits: Hiding malware at kernel level
  • Living off the land: Using legitimate system tools (PowerShell, WMI)
  • Timestomping: Modifying file timestamps to avoid detection

Lateral Movement

  • Pass-the-hash: Using stolen password hashes for authentication
  • Remote services: RDP, SSH, SMB for network traversal
  • Internal spear-phishing: Targeting users from compromised accounts

Collection & Exfiltration

  • Data staging: Aggregating stolen data before exfiltration
  • Compression/encryption: Preparing data for transfer
  • Covert channels: DNS tunneling, steganography, cloud storage abuse

Tracking APT Groups

Naming Conventions

Different security vendors use different naming schemes for the same APT groups:

  • FireEye/Mandiant: APT + number (APT1, APT28, APT29)
  • CrowdStrike: Animal names by nation (Fancy Bear, Cozy Bear, Panda)
  • Microsoft: Chemical elements (Strontium, Yttrium, Zinc)
  • Kaspersky: Descriptive names (Equation Group, Turla)
  • Palo Alto: Numeric designations (Unit 42 naming)

Attribution Challenges

  • False flags: Deliberate use of other groups' tools/TTPs
  • Tool sharing: Malware sold or leaked between groups
  • Proxy operations: Nation-states using cybercriminal contractors
  • Limited visibility: Incomplete telemetry and data

Defending Against APTs

1. Threat Intelligence Integration

  • Subscribe to APT-focused threat intelligence feeds
  • Monitor MITRE ATT&CK framework for APT TTPs
  • Track groups targeting your industry/region
  • Implement indicators of compromise (IOCs) in security tools

2. Detection & Monitoring

  • Deploy EDR (Endpoint Detection & Response) solutions
  • Implement network traffic analysis and anomaly detection
  • Enable comprehensive logging and SIEM correlation
  • Conduct regular threat hunting exercises

3. Preventive Controls

  • Implement application whitelisting
  • Deploy multi-factor authentication (MFA) everywhere
  • Segment networks to limit lateral movement
  • Maintain rigorous patch management
  • Conduct security awareness training (anti-phishing)

4. Incident Response Readiness

  • Develop APT-specific incident response playbooks
  • Conduct tabletop exercises simulating APT scenarios
  • Establish relationships with threat intelligence vendors
  • Prepare forensic capabilities for deep investigations

⚠️ Critical Insight

APT detection requires behavioral analysis, not just signature-based detection. Focus on identifying anomalous patterns, unusual lateral movement, and suspicious data staging activities rather than relying solely on known malware signatures.

Resources for APT Tracking

  • MITRE ATT&CK: Comprehensive knowledge base of APT TTPs
  • APT Groups and Operations: Google Sheets tracker by Florian Roth
  • Threat Actor Encyclopedia: ThaiCERT's comprehensive database
  • APT Notes: Community-driven APT research repository
  • Vendor Threat Reports: FireEye, CrowdStrike, Kaspersky, Microsoft

Conclusion

Understanding APT groups and their methodologies is fundamental to modern cybersecurity. While these adversaries are sophisticated and well-resourced, organizations can significantly reduce risk through:

  • Continuous threat intelligence: Stay informed about relevant APT activity
  • Defense in depth: Layer security controls to increase attacker costs
  • Assume breach mentality: Focus on detection and response, not just prevention
  • Threat-informed defense: Prioritize defenses based on actual APT TTPs

The APT landscape constantly evolves with new groups emerging and existing groups adapting their tactics. Maintaining awareness and adapting defenses accordingly is an ongoing requirement for security-conscious organizations.
